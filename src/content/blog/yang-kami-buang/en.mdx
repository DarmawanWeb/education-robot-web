The first concept drawing of SD v1 was full of nice things: two status lights, a blue button, an OLED face, a distance sensor, and a buzzer. On the poster it all looked good. When we wrote the lessons one by one, some of those parts turned out to have no job.

## Every part needs a lesson

Our rule is simple: a part goes in the box only if a meeting actually uses it.

The status lights lost to the OLED face. The face shows happy, puzzled, alert, and success expressions, and primary students understand an "alert" robot faster than a robot whose yellow light is on.

The button went too. Programs on SD v1 start from the when started block, and an extra button is one more thing to explain before a child can try anything. A light sensor was on the list for a while, then came off because we had no project strong enough for it at primary level.

What is left is two inputs and two outputs. The line and distance sensors read the world; the OLED face and the buzzer answer it. Those four parts are enough for six modules and 32 meetings.

## A battery students never open

The most serious decision was the battery. Primary students never handle loose cells. The battery is enclosed, protected, and only charged with the charger that comes for it. Teachers do the charging.

This part is still being tested on the prototype: temperature after long use, how long the robot lasts in a meeting, and what happens when the battery runs low. Until the results are in, the power specs on this site are described as a design, which is what they are.

## Easy to service

The Arduino Nano on SD v1 sits in a protected carrier and can be pulled out. If it breaks, a teacher swaps it without soldering. School robots are used for years by many classes, so being easy to fix matters as much as any feature.

If SD v1 looks simpler than other kits on the market, that is on purpose. Every part in the box is there because a meeting uses it.

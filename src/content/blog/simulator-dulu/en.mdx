The ideal robotics class has a robot for every student. A real one usually has ten robots for thirty children, one power socket in the corner, and batteries that were last charged a week ago.

That is why every meeting in our curriculum opens in the simulator. Every student can try at the same time, on a laptop or in the computer lab, without queueing for a robot.

## Splitting 70 minutes

For primary classes, this is the split we suggest:

1. Ten minutes to open. The teacher shows the mission and students guess what will happen.
2. Twenty minutes in the simulator. Students build the program and keep trying until the mission works.
3. Thirty minutes with the robot. The same program gets tested on the classroom floor.
4. Ten minutes to reflect. What was different from the simulator, and what did you change?

In this order, time with the robot goes to the things that need a robot: watching, measuring, fixing. Hunting for a block in the wrong place already happened on screen.

## Mistakes are cheap in the simulator

A robot that hits the wall in the simulator restarts with one click. A robot that falls off the desk needs its motors checked, its wheels checked, and sometimes its owner's feelings too.

Children also try strange numbers when they know nothing can break. They type 100 percent speed, watch the robot overshoot, and turn it down themselves. That sticks better than a warning from the teacher at the start.

## The parts we left imperfect

A simulator that is too perfect misleads. If the robot on screen always drives straight, students are surprised when the real one drifts.

So in the SMP v1 simulator, the right motor is slightly weaker, like real motors that are never twins. The motion control mission only finishes once students balance the left and right PWM. The distance sensor also has a field of view, so an angled object can read farther away than you expect.

Some things cannot be copied: a slippery floor, a battery running low, sunlight from the window. That is the job of step four. We want students to find the differences themselves and write them down on the worksheet.

## If the school has no robots yet

The simulator is free, runs in the browser, and needs no account. A school can use it for as long as it likes before deciding whether it needs real robots. Teachers who have tried the missions themselves also find it easier to judge which kit suits their class.
